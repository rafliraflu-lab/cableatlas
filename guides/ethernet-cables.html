Cat5e, Cat6, and Ethernet Cable Basics

Choose Ethernet cables for ordinary home networks without paying for unsupported speed claims.

Updated 11 November 2026 · CableAtlas editorial · Device-specific verification required

Key principle: A connection works only when the devices, ports, cables, and adapters support the required feature together. Always verify your specific device model.

Cable category is not your internet speed

A cable category is a performance specification; actual network speed depends on the network interfaces, switches, cable quality and installation.

For a typical home

A well-made Cat5e or Cat6 cable is often adequate for common home network connections. Check the equipment speed and cable run before upgrading.

Length and installation matter

Long runs, tight bends, poor termination and electrical interference can affect performance. Avoid damaged cables and excessive strain.

Watch for misleading listings

Look for reputable specifications, correct conductor material, and suitable installation ratings. Extremely cheap no-name cables can be inconsistent.

Test method

Check the negotiated link speed on the device and switch. Try a known-good short cable before assuming the router or internet plan is at fault.

WORKED EXAMPLE / WHAT TO CHECK

Example: a connection that looks correct but fails

A home router promises gigabit networking, but a computer reports a 100 Mbps Ethernet connection after a cable was run under a door.

What is happening behind the connector?

A negotiated 100 Mbps link can signal a damaged pair, poor termination, an older port or an intentionally limited device. Marketing terms such as Cat7 or Cat8 cannot upgrade the electronics in a router, switch or laptop. Distance, installation quality and interference become more important with higher speeds. For ordinary short home connections, a sound Cat5e or Cat6 cable often matters more than an expensive category printed on a questionable listing.

Reproduce the problem without buying anything

  1. Inspect negotiated link speed in the operating system or router interface rather than relying only on an internet speed test.
  2. Try a short, known-good patch cable directly between the computer and router or switch.
  3. Confirm the Ethernet ports on both devices support the same target speed.
  4. Inspect for crushed, sharply bent or poorly terminated cable; avoid reusing one that is visibly damaged.

How to decide what to replace

Choose a properly manufactured cable matched to the network and installation environment. For permanent building runs, use appropriate in-wall ratings and local code practices rather than repurposing any random patch cord. If several endpoints all negotiate 100 Mbps, diagnose the shared network hardware before replacing every cable.

Primary reference: IEEE Ethernet overview. CableAtlas has not tested a particular brand or device; examples illustrate a method rather than a compatibility guarantee.

Verification checklist

  1. Find the exact model numbers of both devices.
  2. Read the official manufacturer specification for each port.
  3. Check the cable or adapter rating and required direction.
  4. Test a direct connection before adding hubs or docks.
  5. Record what worked so you can repeat the setup.
